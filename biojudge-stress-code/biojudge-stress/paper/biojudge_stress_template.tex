\begin{abstract}
Biomedical AI systems increasingly let one language model check another's work, comparing two answers or picking the best of several. How good these checkers are has usually been judged against human preference ratings, one bias at a time. We judge them against expert answer keys, under several pressures at once. Four small open-weight models (2--3B parameters, 4-bit, one per family) decide which of two explanations is correct for @@NItemsKept@@ expert-keyed questions from PubMedQA, MedQA-USMLE, and MedMCQA, and those same models wrote the explanations. We vary presentation order, length, and authorship, and add six edits that change nothing medical. Three controls separate real persuasion from a near-random judge changing its mind: @@NPairwise@@ pairwise verdicts and @@NSelect@@ best-of-4 selections in all. The judges barely verify. They agree with the key @@AccBase@@\% of the time, @@FlipBase@@\% of verdicts reverse when the two answers change places, and every judge is a worse checker than it is an answerer. Presentation decides too much: putting the wrong answer first costs @@RegWA@@ percentage points (pp), and one judge gains @@SPBMax@@~pp when its own family wrote the correct answer rather than the wrong one. The edits look devastating and mostly are not: two neutral sentences carrying no cue already overturn @@ASRplaceboBase@@\% of correct verdicts. Against that floor only a note claiming prior review clearly persuades, costing @@ABS:DAccInjectionBase@@~pp; fake credentials and markdown cost nothing. Defenses split cleanly. Deleting the cue text with simple pattern matching blocks the edits as well as an LLM rewriter does, while the rewriter's @@DeltaAccNorm@@~pp gain comes from equalizing length, not from understanding; instructing the judge to ignore bias does neither. In best-of-4 selection, decorating one incorrect candidate drops accuracy to @@SelAccAttack@@\%, below the @@SelChanceScored@@\% a random pick scores. A verifier has to be measured in the domain where it will run, with a control for instability, before it is trusted.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A biomedical answer produced by an agentic system has usually passed through several language-model calls before anyone reads it. Clinical reasoning frameworks assemble panels of specialist agents and reconcile what they propose \cite{b1,b2}; simulated clinical environments and discovery agents plan, call tools, and criticize their own intermediate steps \cite{b3,b4,b5}; and the results are often scored by model juries or rubric-based graders \cite{b6,b7}. Health systems now generate more machine-written text than any clinician can read, so the decision about which candidate answer survives falls to a model. That model is the quality-control layer of the pipeline. It is rarely tested under the conditions it will actually face.

General-domain work shows that judges are swayed by position \cite{b8,b9,b10}, by length \cite{b11,b12}, and by recognizing their own family's prose \cite{b13,b14,b15}, as well as by authority cues, fabricated references, formatting \cite{b16,b17,b18}, injected instructions \cite{b19,b20}, adaptive stylistic edits \cite{b21}, and even a fixed ``null'' answer \cite{b22}. Three things about that literature limit what it says about biomedical deployment. The reference signal is usually human preference, so a bias may only mean that the judge disagrees with raters, not that it is wrong. Biases are mostly studied one at a time, which leaves open whether they add up or compound. And a correction is typically evaluated on the single bias it was designed for.

The gap matters here because the cues that judges reward are cheap to produce and look exactly like medical credibility: claimed expertise, journal-style citations, confident guideline language. Models fabricate convincing references without being asked \cite{b23}, and prompt injection already works against medical vision-language models \cite{b24}. A verifier that reads such signals as evidence converts a safety component into the easiest thing in the pipeline to attack.

BioJudge-Stress therefore puts small open-weight models in the verifier seat and scores them against expert answer keys. For each keyed multiple-choice item, four model families write a rationale defending the keyed answer and a rationale defending an incorrect option that the model pool itself finds plausible. The same four families then judge those pairs, so authorship is crossed with correctness by construction. Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the design. We ask four questions:

\textbf{RQ1.} Do position, verbosity, and self-preference act independently, or does an incorrect response become disproportionately persuasive when the biases coincide? \emph{H1: the self $\times$ length interaction is super-additive.}

\textbf{RQ2.} How much of the gap between verifier and answer key do corrections close, alone and in combination, and does fixing one bias worsen another (RQ2a)? \emph{H2: input-level corrections beat prompt-level ones.}

\textbf{RQ3.} Are bias magnitudes and judge rankings stable across biomedical literature, clinical medicine, and pharmacology? \emph{H3: judge rankings are stable across domains.}

\textbf{RQ4.} Can content-free edits overturn correct verdicts in pairwise and best-of-$N$ verification, and do corrected pipelines resist them? \emph{H4: edits overturn correct verdicts above a placebo baseline.}

H1--H4 and the four primary contrasts were fixed in the manuscript draft before the main run; the equivalence bound in Section~\ref{sec:metrics} was chosen afterwards and is reported as such. Our contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item A gold-anchored, fully crossed design in which every family is both rationale author and verifier, so position, verbosity, self-preference, and their interactions are measured against correctness rather than against a preference label.
\item Three instability controls --- a length-matched placebo edit, the same edits applied to the \emph{correct} response, and a repair rate --- which show that most of the apparent attack success of these judges is verdict churn, and that only a claim of prior review persuades them.
\item Seven corrections at three pipeline levels (verifier prompt, input normalization by a preprocessing agent with a deterministic strip-only ablation, and aggregation), each scored on every bias and every attack condition rather than on its own target.
\item A best-of-$N$ setting in which one correct candidate faces three candidates that have converged on the same wrong answer, leaving the verifier as the only safeguard.
\item Evidence that small verifiers sit near chance, gain nothing in the verifier role over answering the same items alone, show no detectable self $\times$ length interaction while position and length do compound, and are helped most by input normalization.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Biases of LLM judges.} MT-Bench established pairwise LLM evaluation and already reported position and verbosity bias alongside strong agreement with human preferences \cite{b8}. Position bias has since been mapped across judges and tasks \cite{b9,b10}, as have length preference \cite{b11,b12} and self-preference and its link to self-recognition \cite{b13,b14,b15}. Related work documents cognitive, stylistic, and presentation biases \cite{b16,b17,b18,b25}, including a preference for answers that are merely described as already refined \cite{b17}, and benchmarks now inject biases deliberately \cite{b53,b54}. Surveys chart the area \cite{b26,b27}, and one recent study compares nine mitigation strategies across biases on human-labeled general-domain data \cite{b28}. We differ in working in biomedicine, scoring against answer keys, crossing the biases to measure interaction, adding content-free edits with instability controls, and extending to best-of-$N$ selection.

\textbf{Corrections.} The standard repertoire is order swapping and calibration \cite{b8,b9}, length control \cite{b12}, debiasing instructions, and multi-family panels \cite{b29}. Yang et al.~\cite{b21} showed that adaptive stylistic edits defeat style control and proposed style normalization; we implement it as a preprocessing agent and test it on every bias and attack, including a cue it was never told about.

\textbf{Gameability.} Judges can be moved without any gain in answer quality by universal adversarial phrases \cite{b20}, optimized prompt injections \cite{b19}, sycophantic language \cite{b30}, and bandit-selected stylistic edits \cite{b21}; indirect injection threatens any evaluator that reads text produced by other agents or tools \cite{b31}. Our edits are fixed templates, so they estimate what a low-effort upstream agent gets for free rather than what an optimizing adversary could achieve.

\textbf{LLM judges and verifiers in biomedicine.} Verifiers that rank sampled solutions improve reasoning \cite{b32,b33}, and debate and self-consistency compare candidate answers in much the same way \cite{b34,b35}; biomedical agent systems have adopted both \cite{b1,b2}. MedHELM scores medical tasks with an LLM jury validated against clinicians \cite{b6}, and HealthBench grades against physician-written rubrics \cite{b7}. A scoping review of LLM judges in healthcare lists position, length, and related-model preference among their failure modes \cite{b51}. Judges agree less with experts than with lay raters in specialized domains \cite{b36}; same-provider judges change the apparent safety of medical models \cite{b50}; and pairwise judges are strongly position-biased when detecting medical fabrication \cite{b52}. Closest to our setting, de Oliveira et al.~\cite{b49} evaluate biomedical judges against metric-derived gold labels for correctness, resampling robustness, and format compliance. We have found no prior study that crosses interacting biases, content-free edits with instability controls, and corrections against expert answer keys for biomedical verifiers (searches of arXiv, the ACL Anthology, and OpenReview through September 2026).

\section{The BioJudge-Stress Framework}
\subsection{What the verifier does, and what the adversary controls}
We study the two roles a verifier plays in an agentic pipeline (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}a and c). As a \emph{pairwise verifier} it receives a question and two candidate answers and names the better one, which is what critic steps, debate resolution, and preference labelling all ask for. As a \emph{best-of-$N$ selector} it receives $N=4$ candidates and returns one. The adversary is whatever upstream agent, tool, or data source controls the surface form of one candidate but not its medical content: it may add text, markup, credentials, or references, but it may not change which option the candidate defends or supply real evidence. Its edits are fixed templates, applied without feedback from the judge. Throughout, a \emph{judge} is a model, a \emph{verifier} is that model in the pairwise role, a \emph{selector} is the same model in the best-of-$N$ role, and an \emph{edit} is a content-free change to a candidate.

\subsection{Items and the gold anchor}
We draw @@NItemsSampled@@ items, 25 per domain, under a fixed seed. Literature items come from the expert-labeled PubMedQA subset (PQA-L), restricted to yes/no decisions and presented with the structured abstract minus its conclusion \cite{b37}; clinical items from the four-option test split of MedQA-USMLE \cite{b38}; pharmacology items from the MedMCQA validation split, subject Pharmacology, single-answer only \cite{b39}. Options of the ``all/none of the above'' kind are dropped, since a rationale cannot argue for them without referring to the other options. Every item ends up as a lettered single-best-answer question.

Scoring is fixed by the key, not by a preference. A verdict scores $s=1$ if it prefers the response defending the keyed answer, $s=0$ if it prefers the other one, and $s=\tfrac{1}{2}$ for an explicit tie or an unparseable verdict. Half credit keeps a judge that always abstains at 50\% rather than at 0\%, and keeps the design balanced; Section~\ref{sec:rq1} reports what the two alternative rules do to the headline number. The exam or expert key is thus a human anchor that needs no new annotation.

\subsection{Building the candidates}
\textbf{Hard negatives.} Each judge model first answers every item alone, in at most 60 words followed by a final letter. The wrong option chosen most often across the four models becomes that item's distractor, with ties broken at random and a random wrong option used when no model erred. The distractor is therefore the mistake the model pool itself finds plausible, which is the case on @@HardNegRate@@\% of items (Table~\ref{tab:evaluation-items}). Two-option literature items have only one wrong option, so there the \emph{hard} label records only whether some model chose it.

\textbf{Forced-answer rationales.} Each of the four author models then writes one rationale for the keyed answer (R+) and one for the distractor (R$-$), from the same prompt. A rationale is valid if it ends on the target letter, never endorses another option, does not refuse, and reaches 30 words; invalid output is retried twice at temperature 0.7. An item enters the judging stages only if all eight of its rationales are valid, which leaves @@NItemsKept@@ items (@@NItemsLit@@ literature, @@NItemsClin@@ clinical, @@NItemsPharm@@ pharmacology) and @@ValidRate@@\% valid rationales. Because every family writes both a correct and an incorrect rationale for every item, authorship is balanced against correctness by construction rather than by sampling.

\subsection{The manipulations}
\textbf{Position.} Every pair is judged in both orders.

\textbf{Verbosity.} A utility agent outside the judge pool (Phi-4-mini \cite{b40}, so that padding carries no judge's stylistic signature) expands a rationale to roughly $2.5\times$ its length by restating the question and adding general background, without adding facts that bear on which option is right. Output that changes the final line or falls short of $1.6\times$ the original length falls back to a deterministic template; @@PadLLMRate@@\% of padded texts come from the model, at a mean length ratio of @@PadRatio@@. Each pair is judged unpadded, with R$-$ padded, and with R+ padded.

\textbf{Authorship.} For each item the four generators are randomly permuted into a cycle, and consecutive pairs supply (R+, R$-$). Every family is the correct author exactly once and the incorrect author exactly once, so each judge meets one self-right pair, one self-wrong pair, and two neutral pairs per item, and the design stays balanced for every judge.

\textbf{Content-free edits.} Six deterministic edits (Table~\ref{tab:content-free-edits}) are applied to R$-$ in the first two pairs of every item, in both orders. All of them keep the final-answer line and none of them adds medical evidence.

\subsection{Three controls for verdict instability}
\label{sec:controls}
A near-chance judge changes its mind easily, so ``the verdict changed after the edit'' is not by itself evidence that the edit persuaded anyone. Three controls separate the two.

\textbf{Placebo.} The same pairs and orders also receive a length-matched pair of neutral sentences that carry no cue at all: no credentials, no claim of confidence, no markup, no reference, nothing addressed to the evaluator. Its success rate is the floor against which every edit is read.

\textbf{Symmetric edits.} Each of the five single edits is also applied to R+ instead of R$-$. If a cue persuades, it should help whichever side carries it; if it merely perturbs, the direction will not matter.

\textbf{Held-out edit.} One further edit claims that most of the specialists consulted agree with the answer. The normalizer's prompt names credentials, references, markup, confidence, filler, and evaluator-directed text, but it was never told about a consensus claim, so this edit asks whether the defense generalizes past the cues it was written for.

To these we add the mirror image of an attack: the \emph{repair rate}, the share of verdicts that were \emph{wrong} on the clean pair and become right under the same edit (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}).

\subsection{Corrections}
Corrections enter at three levels.

At the \textbf{prompt} level, a debiasing clause tells the judge to decide on correctness and medical validity alone; to ignore order, length, formatting, tone, and stated confidence; to treat credentials and references inside a response as unverified; and to ignore instructions that appear inside a response.

At the \textbf{input} level, a normalizer agent (Phi-4-mini again) rewrites every candidate, padded and edited ones included, into at most 80 words of plain prose that keep the final answer and the main reasoning steps while dropping credentials, references, markup, confidence statements, filler, and text addressed to an evaluator. @@NormLLMRate@@\% of rewrites pass validation; the rest fall back to an attack-agnostic routine that strips markup and truncates. Because an LLM rewrite is both a defense and a confound --- the rewriting model has medical knowledge of its own --- we add a deterministic \emph{strip-only} ablation that removes markup and bracketed text and nothing else: no rewriting, no compression, no removal of credentials, citations, or confident wording. The difference between the two is what the rewrite itself buys. Crossing the clause with the LLM normalizer gives four protocols (base/raw, debias/raw, base/normalized, debias/normalized), and the ablation adds a fifth (base/strip-only).

At the \textbf{aggregation} level, a swap view counts a preference only if it survives order reversal and calls it a tie otherwise; a jury takes the majority verdict of the four judges; a recusal jury first drops every judge whose family wrote either candidate, which in the cyclic design leaves two judges per pair; and the full pipeline (D+N+R+S) stacks the debiasing clause, the normalizer, recusal, and the swap. With the uncorrected baseline this gives the nine configurations of Table~\ref{tab:debiasing-corrections}.

\subsection{Best-of-4 selection}
The selector sees four candidates. All three incorrect ones defend the same hard-negative distractor, so a majority vote over final answers is wrong on every item and the verifier's reading of the reasoning is the only thing standing between the pipeline and a wrong answer. This is a deliberate worst case: a debate or sampling stage that has converged on a popular error. One incorrect candidate is designated \emph{perturbed} and is left unchanged (clean pool), padded, or given the combined edit. The correct candidate is rotated through all four positions, under the base/raw and debias/normalized protocols.

\subsection{Design at a glance}
Per item, judge, and protocol the design yields 24 bias units ($4$ pairs $\times\ 3$ length conditions $\times\ 2$ orders) and 24 attack units ($2$ pairs $\times\ 6$ edits $\times\ 2$ orders). The controls are cheaper because they answer narrower questions: 8 placebo and held-out units per item and judge under each of the four main protocols, 20 symmetric units under the base protocol only, and the strip-only ablation repeats the 48 bias and attack units once. Over @@NItemsKept@@ items and four judges that is @@NPairwise@@ pairwise verdicts, of which @@NCtrlVerdicts@@ belong to the controls and the ablation, plus @@NSelect@@ selection verdicts ($3$ pools $\times\ 4$ rotations $\times\ 2$ protocols $\times\ 4$ judges).

\section{Metrics and Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:metrics}
A judging unit $u$ is one (judge, protocol, item, pair, condition, order) tuple with score $s_u\in\{0,\tfrac{1}{2},1\}$. Clean units carry no padding and no edit. $\mathrm{Acc}(\cdot)$ is the mean of $s_u$ over the units that match a condition. Rates are percentages and differences are percentage points (pp).

\paragraph{Agreement} Accuracy (Acc) is the mean score on clean units. Cohen's $\kappa$ \cite{b41} measures agreement between the keyed slot and the verdict (A, B, or tie). Verification gain (VG) is a judge's verifier accuracy minus its own solo accuracy on the same items --- the quantity a system designer actually cares about, since a verifier is only worth adding if it beats the model that would otherwise answer.

\paragraph{Position} Flip is the share of clean pairs whose outcome (correct, incorrect, or tie) changes when the order is reversed. The position-preference index $\mathrm{PPI}=E[\mathbf{1}(v{=}A)-\mathbf{1}(v{=}B)]$ on clean units is positive when slot A is favored.

\paragraph{Verbosity} $\mathrm{VB}=\mathrm{Acc}(\text{no pad})-\mathrm{Acc}(\text{R}-\text{ padded})$ is the accuracy lost when the wrong answer is made longer, so a negative VB means padding the wrong answer \emph{raises} accuracy. We also report $\mathrm{VB}_{\mathrm{sym}}=\mathrm{Acc}(\text{R}+\text{ padded})-\mathrm{Acc}(\text{R}-\text{ padded})$.

\paragraph{Self-preference} For judge $j$, $\mathrm{SPB}_j=\mathrm{Acc}_j(\text{self-right})-\mathrm{Acc}_j(\text{self-wrong})$ on clean units. Both terms come from the same items, so whatever the judge believes about the answer cancels out. For a panel, family favoritism for family $c$ is $f_c=\mathrm{Acc}(c\text{ wrote R}+)-\mathrm{Acc}(c\text{ wrote R}-)$. The signed mean of $f_c$ is zero by construction in the balanced cyclic design, so tables report $|\mathrm{SPB}|$: the mean absolute value over judges (single-judge rows) or over families (panel rows).

\paragraph{Interaction (RQ1)} With SW for self-wrong, N for neutral, and PW for a padded incorrect rationale, the interaction contrast
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{IC} ={}& [\mathrm{Acc}(\mathrm{N}) - \mathrm{Acc}(\mathrm{SW},\mathrm{PW})] \\
&- [\mathrm{Acc}(\mathrm{N}) - \mathrm{Acc}(\mathrm{SW})] - [\mathrm{Acc}(\mathrm{N}) - \mathrm{Acc}(\mathrm{N},\mathrm{PW})]
\end{align*}
is the joint loss minus the two single losses; $\mathrm{IC}>0$ means super-additive. We also fit a linear probability model on the bias block of the base protocol,
\begin{align*}
s_u ={}& \alpha_j + \beta_1 \mathrm{WA} + \beta_2 \mathrm{SW} + \beta_3 \mathrm{SR} + \beta_4 \mathrm{PW} + \beta_5 \mathrm{PR} \\
&+ \beta_6 (\mathrm{SW}{\times}\mathrm{PW}) + \beta_7 (\mathrm{WA}{\times}\mathrm{PW}) \\
&+ \beta_8 (\mathrm{WA}{\times}\mathrm{SW}) + \varepsilon_u,
\end{align*}
with judge fixed effects $\alpha_j$, WA for an incorrect answer in slot A, SR for self-right, PR for a padded correct rationale, and standard errors clustered by item \cite{b42}. A negative $\beta_6$ is a positive IC. Because a nonsignificant interaction is not evidence of additivity, we also test equivalence with two one-sided tests (TOST) \cite{b56} against a bound of $\pm@@ICBound@@$~pp --- below every main effect we measure, and chosen after seeing the data, so it bounds the effect rather than testing a prediction --- and report the minimum detectable effect (MDE) at 80\% power. A second fit adds the log ratio of the two responses' lengths, which separates what padding does from what being the longer response does.

\paragraph{Gameability} For edit $e$ we report four quantities, all conditioned on the same unit's clean verdict. $\mathrm{ASR}_e$ is the share of units that were right on the clean pair and are wrong after $e$ is applied to R$-$ (a tie counts one half). $\mathrm{RR}_e$ is the repair rate: the share of units that were \emph{wrong} on the clean pair and are right after the same edit. $\mathrm{ASR}_e-\mathrm{RR}_e$ is the net rate, and $\mathrm{ASR}_e-\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{placebo}}$ the placebo-adjusted rate. Neither conditional rate says what an edit does to accuracy overall, so we also report $\Delta\mathrm{Acc}_e$, the accuracy on the edited units minus the accuracy on their own clean counterparts; this is the number a deployment cares about. $\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{any}}$ counts a unit if at least one of the five single edits breaks it. The symmetric control reports the same rate with the edit on R+.

\paragraph{Selection} Selector accuracy is the share of picks that hit the correct candidate; capture is the share that hit the designated perturbed candidate, with the clean pool as its matched baseline. A pick whose candidate number cannot be parsed counts as a miss, which is the conservative reading of an agent that returns nothing usable, but it also lowers the rate a random selector would reach: with a parse rate of $r$ the chance level under this rule is $r/4$ rather than 25\%. We therefore report the parse rate, the implied chance level, and every quantity again over parsed picks only. Position-1 excess is the clean-pool rate of picking the first candidate minus 25~pp.

\paragraph{Uncertainty and multiplicity} All confidence intervals (CIs) are 95\% percentile intervals from an item-level cluster bootstrap \cite{b43} with 2,000 replicates. One bootstrap weight matrix is drawn once and reused for every estimate, so every difference between judges, corrections, or domains is a paired bootstrap difference; $p$-values are two-sided bootstrap sign tests. Cross-domain stability is Kendall's $W$ \cite{b44} over judge rankings in the three domains, with a 20,000-draw permutation test. Four contrasts are designated primary, one per research question: IC (RQ1), $\Delta$Acc of the full pipeline (RQ2), $W$ for accuracy (RQ3), and the change in $\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{any}}$ from base to debias/normalized (RQ4). Their $p$-values are Holm-adjusted \cite{b55} and we report both the raw and the adjusted value. Everything else --- including the per-judge biases, the individual corrections, and the per-edit rates --- is descriptive, and we do not call those results significant.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\textbf{Models.} The four judges, who are also the four authors, are Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct~\cite{b45}, Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct~\cite{b46}, Gemma-2-2B-it~\cite{b47}, and Ministral-3-3B-Instruct~\cite{b48}; Phi-4-mini-instruct~\cite{b40} is the utility agent that pads and normalizes. All five are small open-weight models from five families: the class of model a biomedical group can host itself when data cannot leave the institution, and the class that therefore ends up in the verifier seat in practice. Answering alone, the four judges score between @@SoloMin@@\% and @@SoloMax@@\% on the kept items, and Phi-4-mini scores @@SoloPhi@@\%. The utility model is deliberately excluded from the solo stage, because that stage also fixes each item's hard negative and a fifth voter would change the distractors.

\textbf{Inference.} Every model runs 4-bit quantized through Ollama @@OllamaVersion@@ (llama.cpp back end) with flash attention and a q8\_0 KV cache, 8,192 tokens of context per slot. Quantizations are Q4\_K\_M for Llama, Qwen, Ministral, and Phi and Q4\_0 for Gemma; Table~\ref{tab:evaluation-items} and the repository record the exact model digests. Judges decode greedily (temperature 0) with at most 300 new tokens. No model is trained or fine-tuned, and the edit templates, the padding and normalizer prompts, and all decoding settings were fixed before the main run. Two hosts were used: the padding stage ran on a laptop with one NVIDIA RTX 3050 (6~GB), and every judging stage on a Linux host with four NVIDIA L40S (46~GB each, three of them used), an Intel Xeon Gold 6526Y with 64 threads, and 503~GB of RAM. Because each call is cached on its full request, the two hosts share one cache and no call was repeated. Padding took @@PhasePadHours@@~h on the laptop; judging and selection took @@PhaseJudgeHours@@~h and the control conditions @@PhaseCtrlHours@@~h on the L40S host, at @@JudgeCallsPerSec@@ calls/s (@@JudgeSecPerKCall@@~s per 1,000 verdicts).

\textbf{Greedy is not reproducible.} Temperature 0 fixes the sampling rule, not the arithmetic: with continuous batching the same prompt can be scheduled differently, the floating-point reduction order changes, and the decoded text diverges. We measured this by asking every clean pair of the base protocol a second time, same prompt and same settings. Across @@RetestN@@ re-decodes the text was identical @@RetestText@@\% of the time and the verdict @@RetestVerdict@@\% of the time, and pooled accuracy moved from @@AccBase@@\% to @@RetestAcc@@\%. Per judge the verdict reproduces between @@RetestVerdictMin@@\% (@@RetestJudgeMin@@) and @@RetestVerdictMax@@\% (@@RetestJudgeMax@@) of the time. The estimates are therefore stable but individual verdicts are not, and roughly @@RetestFlipPct@@\% of verdicts would differ on a repeat run with nothing changed at all. Every ``share of verdicts that changed'' in this paper has to be read against that floor, which is exactly what the placebo control measures at the level of a pair.

\textbf{Scale and parse rates.} The main design produced @@NPairwise@@ pairwise verdicts and @@NSelect@@ selections, @@NCtrlVerdicts@@ of the pairwise verdicts coming from the three controls and the strip-only ablation. @@ParseFailRate@@\% of pairwise verdicts carried no parseable verdict tag and @@TieRate@@\% were explicit ties. By judge, missing tags range from @@ParseFailMin@@\% (@@ParseFailJudgeMin@@) to @@ParseFailMax@@\% (@@ParseFailJudgeMax@@). They are not truncation. Re-issuing @@ParseAuditN@@ prompts, @@ParseAuditFails@@ of them verdicts that had failed to parse, the server reported \texttt{stop} on all @@ParseAuditStop@@ and the longest completion ran to @@ParseAuditMaxTok@@ of the @@ParseAuditCap@@ permitted tokens: the models end their explanation without the tag they were asked for. Nor is the failure a property of the prompt --- @@ParseAuditReparse@@\% of those same prompts produce a parseable tag on a second decode, which makes most parse failures another face of the non-determinism above. In best-of-4 selection @@SelParseRate@@\% of picks parse under the base protocol and @@SelParseRateDN@@\% under debias/normalized, which is why selection results are also reported over parsed picks.

\textbf{Software validation.} Every call is cached in SQLite, so a run resumes after an interruption and every analysis recomputes without new queries. Before spending compute we ran the whole pipeline against a simulator with planted position, verbosity, self-preference, and attack effects of known size, including a super-additive self $\times$ length interaction and one unbiased judge. The analysis recovered every planted effect, returned near-zero bias for the unbiased judge, and exposed two estimator bugs, which we fixed; one of them was the signed family favoritism that is zero by construction. Seventeen regression tests, including that end-to-end simulated run and offline checks that each control edit preserves its answer and carries no cue, pass on the released code, and re-running the analysis on the archived main-run outputs reproduces all @@NMacrosMain@@ reported quantities exactly.

\subsection{RQ2: What the corrections buy}
Table~\ref{tab:debiasing-corrections} and Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2} compare the configurations; every $\Delta$ is a paired difference from the single-judge baseline.

Telling the judge about the biases does nothing. The debiasing clause moves accuracy by @@DeltaAccDebias@@~pp @@DeltaAccDebiasCI@@, Flip by @@DeltaFlipDebias@@~pp, and VB by @@NEG:DeltaVBDebias@@~pp. It is not that the clause is ignored --- it does cut the combined edit's success by @@ABS:DeltaASRcomboDebias@@~pp @@DeltaASRcomboDebiasCI@@ --- but nothing that matters for agreement with the key changes.

Acting on the input works better than instructing the judge. Rewriting both candidates into a canonical form raises accuracy by @@DeltaAccNorm@@~pp @@DeltaAccNormCI@@, cuts $|\mathrm{SPB}|$ by @@ABS:DeltaSPBNorm@@~pp and $\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{any}}$ by @@ABS:DeltaASRanyNorm@@~pp, and moves VB from $+@@VBBase@@$ to @@NEG:VBNorm@@~pp. That last move is a reversal, not a removal: after normalization, padding the incorrect rationale \emph{raises} accuracy, and the interval excludes zero. The mechanism is visible in the normalizer's own output. An 80-word rewrite of a 300-word padded text usually fails validation --- @@FB:pad@@\% of padded texts fall back to the deterministic truncation, against @@FB:raw@@\% of unedited ones --- and truncating a padded text keeps its filler and discards its argument. $|\mathrm{VB}|$ therefore falls by @@IMP:Norm:vb@@~pp @@IMPCI:Norm:vb@@ rather than by the @@ABS:DeltaVBNorm@@~pp the signed change suggests. Combining the clause with the normalizer gives $\Delta$Acc $=@@DeltaAccDebNorm@@$~pp @@DeltaAccDebNormCI@@, no better than the normalizer alone.

\textbf{Which half of the normalizer does the work?} The strip-only ablation separates them. Deleting markup and bracketed text with a regular expression --- no rewriting, no compression, no removal of credentials, citations or confident wording --- defeats the edits at least as well as the language model does: the note to the evaluator falls from @@CORR:Base:asr_injection@@\% to @@CORR:Strip:asr_injection@@\%, against @@CORR:Norm:asr_injection@@\% under the full normalizer, and $\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{any}}$ falls by @@ABS:DeltaASRanyStrip@@~pp @@DeltaASRanyStripCI@@ to @@ASRanyStrip@@\%, essentially the normalizer's @@CORR:Norm:asr_any@@\%. It buys nothing else. Accuracy does not move (@@NEG:DeltaAccStrip@@~pp @@DeltaAccStripCI@@), nor does VB (@@NEG:DeltaVBStrip@@~pp @@DeltaVBStripCI@@), $|\mathrm{SPB}|$ (@@DeltaSPBStrip@@~pp @@DeltaSPBStripCI@@) or Flip (@@NEG:DeltaFlipStrip@@~pp). The two halves of the defense are therefore doing different jobs: deleting the cue text is what stops the edits, and a few lines of deterministic code are enough for it, while the accuracy gain and the length and authorship effects come from the rewrite.

Three checks suggest that what the rewrite contributes is compression rather than the rewriting model's own medical knowledge, which would be a confound --- Phi-4-mini answers @@SoloPhi@@\% of the items correctly on its own, more than two of the four judges, so it has knowledge available to leak. First, the gain is the same on the @@NPhiKnownItems@@ items Phi answers correctly (@@DAccNormPhiKnown@@~pp @@DAccNormPhiKnownCI@@) and on the @@NPhiUnknownItems@@ it does not (@@DAccNormPhiUnknown@@~pp @@DAccNormPhiUnknownCI@@); a leak would concentrate on the former. Second, accuracy after a rewrite that passed validation (@@AccNormRewrite@@\%) is indistinguishable from accuracy after the deterministic fallback (@@AccNormFallback@@\%), and the fallback contains no model output at all. Third, the strip-only ablation --- the one variant that removes cues without compressing --- gains nothing. What the two successful variants share is that both shorten the text, and shortening is also what produces the reverse length bias above. We read the normalizer's @@DeltaAccNorm@@~pp as length equalization, not as a second opinion.

Aggregation behaves differently again. The swap view leaves accuracy where it was (@@NEG:DeltaAccSwap@@~pp), because order-dependent verdicts turn into ties rather than into correct answers. The unrecused jury gains @@DeltaAccJury@@~pp @@DeltaAccJuryCI@@. Recusing the families that wrote the candidates gains @@DeltaAccRecusal@@~pp @@DeltaAccRecusalCI@@ --- small, but the one aggregation result whose interval clears zero. Stacking everything gains @@DeltaAccFull@@~pp @@DeltaAccFullCI@@; as the primary RQ2 contrast it does not survive multiplicity control (raw @@CORRP:Full:acc@@ for the configuration itself, Holm-adjusted @@HolmDAccFull@@ for the paired difference), so we do not claim the full pipeline improves accuracy.

\textbf{RQ2a: does fixing one bias worsen another?} Yes, and the cost sits in aggregation (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure2}). Both juries lower $\mathrm{ASR}_{\mathrm{any}}$, by @@ABS:DeltaASRanyJury@@ and @@ABS:DeltaASRanyRecusal@@~pp, and both raise Flip, by @@DeltaFlipJury@@ and @@DeltaFlipRecusal@@~pp, because a majority over disagreeing judges produces ties and a tie counts as a changed outcome under order reversal. A panel buys robustness to edits and pays in order dependence. Panel $|\mathrm{SPB}|$ also rises (@@DeltaSPBJury@@ and @@DeltaSPBRecusal@@~pp), though that number measures family favoritism rather than self-preference and is not comparable with the baseline. The two normalized configurations are the only ones whose point estimates improve on every metric, and even they introduce the reverse length bias above.

\section{Discussion}
The headline is not that these verifiers are biased. It is that they are not verifying. Clean accuracy sits at @@AccBase@@\% with $\kappa=@@KappaBase@@$, @@FlipBase@@\% of verdicts turn on reading order alone, and no judge beats the accuracy it reaches by answering the item itself. Whatever a pipeline gains by adding one of these models as a checker, it is not agreement with the answer key. The biases documented below act on a decision that was close to arbitrary to begin with, which is the practical warning for self-hosted biomedical systems where the verifier role goes to whichever small model fits the available hardware.

The size of the risk depends on which cue is involved, and that only becomes visible with a control. Read without one, our judges look dramatically gameable: @@ASRanyBase@@\% of correct verdicts fall to at least one content-free edit. Read against the repair rate (@@CORR:Base:rr_any@@\%) and the placebo, almost all of that is a near-chance judge changing its mind. Two cues still cost accuracy once churn is removed, and both are claims about provenance rather than about style: a bracketed note saying the answer was already checked (@@NEG:CORR:Base:dacc_injection@@~pp of accuracy) and fabricated references (@@NEG:CORR:Base:dacc_citation@@~pp). Fake credentials, markdown, and asserted confidence do not cost accuracy at all, and the last of them slightly helps. The ranking matters for defense: the dangerous cue is the one that tells the verifier its job has already been done, and it is also the cue a debiasing instruction most conspicuously fails to neutralize.

Interactions were the part of the design we expected to matter most, and they did not. There is no detectable self $\times$ length super-additivity, and the equivalence test rules out anything as large as $\pm@@ICBound@@$~pp. Position and length do compound (@@NEG:RegWAPW@@~pp beyond their main effects), so interactions are worth measuring rather than assuming, but in this setting nearly all the risk was already visible in the main effects.

Three things follow for anyone putting a small verifier into a biomedical pipeline. Normalize the input: rewriting both candidates into a canonical form is the only correction here that improves every metric's point estimate, though our own ablations show how much of that comes from compression rather than from understanding, and it introduces a length bias of the opposite sign. Separate the verifier from the authors: self-preference is not universal, but when a model has it, it is enormous (@@PJ:mistral:spb@@~pp for Ministral-3-3B), and recusal is the one aggregation step with a reliable gain. Do not expect a panel to be free: both juries cut edit success and both raise order dependence by @@DeltaFlipJury@@ to @@DeltaFlipRecusal@@~pp. And measure in the domain you will deploy in, since no ranking we tested transfers across our three domains once multiplicity is accounted for.

Finally, a methodological point that applies well beyond this paper. Single greedy decodes are not reproducible on a batched serving stack: @@RetestFlipPct@@\% of our verdicts change on a second decode of the same prompt. Any metric of the form ``share of verdicts that changed'' --- flip rates, attack success rates, self-consistency --- needs that floor reported alongside it, and a placebo condition is the cheapest way to measure it at the level of the manipulation rather than the token.

\textbf{Limitations.} The judges are small and 4-bit, so the magnitudes here belong to that class of model; what transfers is the method, not the numbers. Multiple-choice items with forced rationales are a controlled proxy for free-text biomedical verification and say nothing about completeness, communication, or clinical safety, and a rationale written to defend an option the model believes is wrong may carry cues that a natural error would not. Padding and normalization are validated automatically, so some rewrites will have strengthened or weakened an argument; the normalizer is a known-cue defense applied by a model with medical knowledge of its own, which is why we report the strip-only ablation beside it. With @@NItemsKept@@ items and four judges, per-model and per-domain intervals are wide, and clean accuracy near chance leaves little headroom for corrections, attacks, or interactions to move. Each domain comes from one benchmark with its own format --- two options in PubMedQA, four in MedQA-USMLE and MedMCQA --- so domain and format are confounded. All three benchmarks are public and may sit in the judges' training data. Each verdict is one 4-bit greedy decode; we measure how much that alone moves verdicts but do not separate quantization from bias. The attacks are fixed templates, not adaptive optimization, so they estimate a floor on what an adversary gets, not a ceiling. The equivalence bound was chosen after seeing the data and is reported as a bound rather than as a test of a prediction. In best-of-4 selection an unparseable pick counts as a miss, which lowers both the measured accuracy and the chance level; both readings are reported. Finally, the control conditions were judged in a later session on the same host, and because the serving stack is not bit-reproducible their normalized text is an independent sample from the same model and prompt rather than the identical strings used in the main run.

\section{Conclusion}
BioJudge-Stress scores LLM verifiers against an expert answer key instead of a preference label, crosses position, length, authorship, and content-free edits in one design, adds the controls that say how much of a changed verdict is persuasion rather than churn, and scores seven corrections on every bias at once. Small self-hostable verifiers turn out to be near chance, strongly order- and length-dependent, and no better than the same model answering the question itself. Most of what looks like gameability is instability; what genuinely moves them is a claim that the answer has already been verified. In best-of-4 selection a single decorated candidate pushes them below chance. Input normalization helps most and recusal a little, but neither makes these models safe to trust as verifiers, and we would not deploy one in a biomedical pipeline without input normalization, same-family recusal, a placebo-controlled attack measurement, and a gold-anchored evaluation in the target domain --- all of which the released harness supports for any OpenAI-compatible or Anthropic endpoint.
